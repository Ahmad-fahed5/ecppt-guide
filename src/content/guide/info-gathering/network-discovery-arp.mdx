## The local network picture

Every host on a network segment is reachable by **IP**, but every frame travels between **MAC addresses**. When your machine wants to talk to `10.10.10.42` on the same segment, it must first find that host's MAC address. That is what ARP does — and it is also why ARP is a perfect discovery tool.

Understanding the local picture — your interfaces, your segment, your neighbors — is the foundation for [Host Discovery](/guides/info-gathering/host-discovery) and for tools like [Netdiscover](/guides/info-gathering/netdiscover).

## What ARP is

The Address Resolution Protocol maps **IP addresses to MAC addresses** on a local segment:

1. Host A wants to reach 10.10.10.42.
2. Host A broadcasts an ARP request: *"Who has 10.10.10.42? Tell 10.10.10.1."*
3. Every host on the segment receives the broadcast; the one with that IP replies with its MAC address.
4. Host A caches the mapping and sends the frame.

Key consequences for discovery:

- **Every alive host on the segment must answer ARP** to function at all — even hosts that filter ICMP and TCP. A host that ignores ping still answers ARP.
- **ARP works only within the local broadcast domain** (your segment). It cannot discover hosts behind a router.
- **ARP requests are broadcast**, so an attacker (in an authorized lab) can see which addresses respond without any port scan.

## Broadcast domains and why they matter

A broadcast domain is the set of hosts that receive each other's broadcast frames — typically everything on the same switch segment (or VLAN). ARP requests and broadcasts do not cross routers by default.

Implications:

- ARP-based discovery is limited to your broadcast domain.
- If a lab has multiple subnets, you need to scan each from a position inside it (or route through it after a foothold — see [Pivoting](/guides/pivoting) later).
- When you gain a foothold on a compromised host (in a training scenario), that host's local segment becomes your next ARP discovery scope.

## Know your own interfaces first

Before scanning anything, map your own network position:

<CodeBlock
  lang="bash"
  title="List all interfaces and addresses"
  code={`ip a`}
/>

<CodeBlock
  lang="text"
  title="Example output"
  code={`1: lo: <LOOPBACK,UP,LOWER_UP> ...
2: eth0: <BROADCAST,MULTICAST,UP,LOWER_UP> mtu 1500 ...
    inet 10.10.14.5/24 scope global eth0
    inet6 fe80::...
3: tun0: <POINTCARRIER,UP,LOWER_UP> ...
    inet 10.10.10.2/24 scope global tun0`}
/>

The important lines: your IPv4 addresses (`inet`) and their network prefixes (`/24`). In a VPN lab, `tun0` is usually the interface that connects to the target network, while `eth0` may be your own LAN.

Confirm your routing:

<CodeBlock
  lang="bash"
  title="Show the routing table"
  code={`ip route`}
/>

This tells you which networks are directly reachable (routes through `dev tun0`) and which require a gateway. If the target range routes through `tun0`, you are on the right segment for ARP discovery.

## Viewing and manipulating the ARP table

As your machine talks to neighbors, it builds an ARP cache. Check what it has already learned:

<CodeBlock
  lang="bash"
  title="Neighbor table"
  code={`ip neigh`}
/>

<CodeBlock
  lang="bash"
  title="Classic ARP table"
  code={`arp -a`}
/>

Both show IP → MAC mappings and their state (`REACHABLE`, `STALE`, `FAILED`). A `REACHABLE` entry is a confirmed live host — a useful and completely passive way to start building your host list before you scan anything.

## Passive vs active local discovery

- **Passive:** listen and collect ARP traffic already on the wire. Tools like `tcpdump` and netdiscover's passive mode (`netdiscover -p`) watch for address claims from all hosts.
- **Active:** send your own ARP requests to every address in the range (an ARP sweep). Nmap does this automatically with `-sn` on the local segment; netdiscover does it with `-r`.

Active ARP sweeps are fast and reliable precisely because every live host must answer. On a local segment they are usually the best first move.

<CodeBlock
  lang="bash"
  title="Quick ARP sweep"
  code={`nmap -sn -PR 10.10.10.0/24`}
/>

## Practical discovery commands

<CodeBlock
  lang="bash"
  title="Neighbor discovery on the segment"
  code={`# What have we already learned?
ip neigh

# Ping sweep forces ARP lookups (and populates the cache)
nmap -sn 10.10.10.0/24

# Check the cache again — now populated with MACs
arp -a`}
/>

Put together, the sequence builds the local map: your interfaces (`ip a`), your reachable networks (`ip route`), your confirmed neighbors (`ip neigh`), and finally a full sweep of the segment.

## The vendor trick

MAC addresses contain an **OUI** — the first three octets identify the vendor (for example `00:50:56` is VMware, `08:00:27` is VirtualBox, `00:15:5d` is Hyper-V). Tools like netdiscover and Nmap will label MACs with their vendor. In a lab this can instantly reveal which hosts are VMs and often which are the actual targets vs. infrastructure.

## Command reference

| Command | Purpose | Expected result | Investigate next |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ip a` | Show interfaces and addresses | Your network position | Choose the right interface to scan |
| `ip route` | Show routing | Which networks are reachable | Scan directly reachable segments |
| `ip neigh` | Show the ARP/neighbor cache | Confirmed live neighbors + MACs | Port scan the confirmed hosts |
| `arp -a` | Show ARP cache (classic) | Same as `ip neigh` | Cross-check MAC vendors |
| `nmap -sn -PR RANGE` | Active ARP sweep | All live hosts on the segment | Full port scan survivors |
| `netdiscover -r RANGE` | ARP sweep with vendor labels | Hosts + MAC vendors | Identify VMs/targets |

## Common mistakes

- **Running ARP-based tools against remote networks** (behind a router) where they cannot work.
- **Forgetting to check `ip a`/`ip route` first** and scanning through the wrong interface.
- **Ignoring the ARP cache** — it is free, passive intelligence you already have.
- **Treating MAC vendors as definitive OS identification.** The vendor tells you the NIC manufacturer, not the operating system.
- **Assuming one broadcast domain.** Multiple segments require discovery from each.
